Welcome to the troop

What is Scouts BSA?

Scouts BSA is Scouting America’s traditional program for youth ages 11–17. Scouts learn by planning activities, solving problems, serving their communities, and taking increasing responsibility in a troop.

Who can join? Youth may join at age 11 and remain through age 17. Some 10-year-olds are eligible after earning the Arrow of Light or after March 1 of their fifth-grade year. Capitol Area Council has troops for boys and troops for girls across its 15 counties.

How a troop works

  • Patrols: small teams of Scouts learn and work together
  • Youth leaders: elected Scouts lead meetings and help plan the program
  • Adult guidance: Scoutmasters coach, protect, and support youth leadership
  • Regular rhythm: troops typically meet weekly and add outdoor trips and service projects

Advancement

Grow from new Scout to Eagle.

Advancement is one part of the experience—not a race. Scouts progress by learning skills, showing leadership, serving, and completing requirements at their own pace before age 18.

Rank 1ScoutLearn the foundations of troop life
Rank 2TenderfootBegin building core outdoor skills
Rank 3Second ClassPractice skills with more independence
Rank 4First ClassBecome a capable, contributing Scout
Rank 5StarExpand leadership, service, and interests
Rank 6LifePrepare for the final Eagle journey
Highest rankEagle ScoutComplete a significant service project and all requirements

Family questions

Know what to expect.

Does my child need to have been a Cub Scout?

No. Youth can join Scouts BSA without previous Scouting experience as long as they meet the program’s age requirements.

Are troops coed?

Scouts BSA serves both boys and girls. Local troops are organized as troops for boys or troops for girls. The BeAScout listing shows which youth a troop serves.

What does it cost?

Costs can include national membership, troop dues, uniforms, activities, and camp. Ask each troop for its current annual estimate. Fundraising and assistance may help with membership, uniform, and camp expenses; the council’s Scouts BSA Basics page has more detail.

What is the parent’s role?

Adults provide transportation, logistics, mentoring, and safety support, but the program is planned and led mainly by Scouts. Ask the Scoutmaster how families can observe, volunteer, or support the troop.
